Mary's Room: A Chromatic Qualia Experiment

The Thought Experiment

Mary is a brilliant scientist who lives in a black-and-white room. She has never seen colors, but she knows everything there is to know about color scientifically: wavelengths, neural processing, physical properties—everything that can be expressed in facts and figures.

The philosophical question posed by Frank Jackson (1982): When Mary finally leaves her room and sees color for the first time, does she learn something new?

"It seems, however, that Mary does not know all there is to know. For when she is let out of the black-and-white room or given a color television, she will learn what it is like to see something red, say. This is rightly described as learning—she will not say 'ho, hum.'"

— Frank Jackson, "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982)

This Experiment

In this interactive experience, you will take Mary's place. But instead of seeing colors, you will learn to identify them through sound alone. Each color has been mapped to unique acoustic properties:

  • Hue → Frequency (Red is low, Purple is high)
  • Saturation → Harmonic richness (more saturated = richer harmonics)
  • Brightness → Volume/amplitude

You will train to identify colors by sound, then face the critical moment: when you finally see the actual colors, will you learn something fundamentally new, or have you already captured everything there is to know through the sonic mappings?

About This Experiment

The Philosophy

Mary's Room is one of the most influential thought experiments in the philosophy of mind. It challenges physicalism—the view that all facts are physical facts—by suggesting that there are non-physical facts about conscious experience (qualia).

This experiment translates that question into an empirical form: Can complete functional knowledge (identifying colors perfectly through sound) capture phenomenal knowledge (what it's like to see those colors)?

The Science

Research in cross-modal perception has shown that the brain can learn arbitrary sensory substitutions. This experiment uses consistent audio mappings based on:

  • Frequency discrimination (humans can distinguish ~1400 frequency levels)
  • Harmonic complexity (timbre perception)
  • Amplitude perception (loudness)

Choose Your Audio Synthesis Method

Different people perceive and remember sounds differently. Choose the synthesis method that feels most intuitive to you:

Pure Tones

Simple sine waves. Clean, abstract, minimal. Best for pitch discrimination.

Harmonic Rich

Multiple harmonics. More musical, complex timbres. Easier to distinguish.

FM Synthesis

Frequency modulation. Rich, bell-like tones. Most distinctive character.

You can change this later if needed. The key is consistency—whichever you choose, the same colors will always produce the same sounds.
